# Research behind the design

We want students to spend less effort remembering, organizing, and switching between competing tasks. The evidence below motivates that direction. It does not measure this application's effectiveness.

## Student stress: why the problem matters

**Finding:** 76.4% of students surveyed in the ACHA Spring 2024 reference group reported moderate or high stress during the preceding 30 days.

**Source:** [American College Health Association, National College Health Assessment](https://www.acha.org/ncha/), Spring 2024 reference group highlight.

**Design implication:** Offer a calm overview and a manageable next step, instead of treating every task as equally urgent.

**Limit:** A reference-group survey describes its respondents. It is not a universal prevalence estimate, does not establish that fragmented productivity tools caused stress, and is not evidence that this app reduces it.

## Time management and wellbeing

**Finding:** A 2021 meta-analysis included 158 studies and 53,957 participants. Time management showed moderate relationships with academic achievement and wellbeing. The authors found wellbeing was at least as important a part of the story as performance.

**Source:** Aeon, B., Faber, A., & Panaccio, A. (2021). [Does time management work? A meta-analysis.](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0245066) PLOS ONE, 16(1), e0245066.

**Design implication:** Connect tasks to available time, and leave visible space for rest and life outside school. Planning should support a sustainable day, not just maximize completed tasks.

**Limit:** The review spans different settings and study designs, including correlational research. It does not establish a specific percentage improvement in grades, clarity, or wellbeing, and it does not evaluate Clash of Plans.

## Specific plans and mental interference

**Finding:** Across experiments, unfinished goals interfered with unrelated tasks. Formulating specific plans reduced those interference effects, including intrusive goal-related thoughts.

**Source:** Masicampo, E. J., & Baumeister, R. F. (2011). [Consider it done! Plan making can eliminate the cognitive effects of unfulfilled goals.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21688924/) Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 101(4), 667–683. DOI: 10.1037/a0024192.

**Design implication:** Help turn an open-ended obligation into a concrete task with a duration and a place in the day. This is the basis for our aim of creating more headspace.

**Limit:** These are experimental cognitive-task findings. They are not proof of improved clinical mental health or of long-term academic gains from using an app.

## Focus sessions and task switching

**Finding:** Task-switching experiments show time costs when people alternate between tasks. The APA explains how repeatedly shifting goals and rules can reduce efficiency, particularly for complex tasks.

**Source:** [American Psychological Association: Multitasking—Switching costs](https://www.apa.org/topics/research/multitasking).

**Design implication:** Offer a timer for one task at a time, with configurable breaks. The Pomodoro defaults are a product choice, not a proven optimal interval for every student.

**Limit:** We do not claim that reducing switches between our interface's views produces a measured gain, or that a 25-minute session is universally superior. We intentionally avoid converting the APA's often-quoted “up to 40%” characterization into an app benefit.

## What we can say today

Clash of Plans is **designed to** reduce the effort of juggling tasks and calendars and to help students make concrete plans. Research supports exploring these design choices. We have not measured improvements in mental clarity, stress, academic performance, or productivity among the app's users.

A useful next study would compare common planning workflows with the app, measuring time to create a usable plan, perceived planning effort, task capture accuracy, and usability. Any longer-term wellbeing or academic claims would require a suitable study rather than marketing extrapolation.
